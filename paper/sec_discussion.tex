\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a practitioner should take from this.}
If the aim is long-run control of a risk threshold under prompt shift, use an online update with a calibration window of the right length. The update gives the guarantee (Theorem~\ref{thm:longrun}), and the best window differed between our streams. Among the online rules we tried, DtACI was the strongest on the synthetic streams, on MMLU and on the recurring-regime orderings of RealToxicityPrompts. PromptShift-CRC is a reasonable choice when the right window is unknown: in every setting it did better than the worst window of the Adaptive CRC family, and it was in the better half of the family on MMLU and RealToxicityPrompts. Its computed prompt drift $D_t$ and effective size $n_{\mathrm{eff},t}$ say how far the current prompt is from the evidence behind the threshold. We tested whether the drift predicts violations (Section~\ref{sec:res-trace}) and found that it does not do so reliably, so we describe it as a measure of novelty and not as an alarm.

\paragraph{Why the weights add little.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:longrun} gives the update full responsibility for the long-run rate. The weights can then only improve the \emph{local} rate and the sharpness. Two things limit what they can do. First, a kernel on sentence embeddings groups prompts by topic, and the risk of a prompt depends only weakly on its topic, so a topic-similar past prompt is a weak guide to the current risk; MMLU is the clearest example, since the median risk differs across categories while the upper tail does not. Second, down-weighting costs effective size (Lemma~\ref{lem:m}), and with a threshold at the $0.9$ quantile that cost is paid in the level of the quantile. On RealToxicityPrompts, where the upper tail does move between bands, a longer effective window helps a little. The kernel at the median bandwidth is close to uniform there: a prompt from the highest toxicity band receives \RtpKernelRatio{} times the kernel weight of a prompt from another band, and the effective size of the kernel rule is \RtpKernelNeff{} of a pool of \PoolLen{}. The gain is therefore a window effect and not a similarity effect. A kernel four times sharper does localise, but it does not move post-shift violation by more than about half a percentage point at equal sharpness, with one lead that we do not claim (Section~\ref{sec:res-bw}).

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Scale of the real-output experiments.} We used three small open models (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, GPT-2 and Qwen2.5-0.5B) on CPU, two toxicity classifiers, one prompt template, one sampled continuation per prompt, and one embedding model. Larger models, other templates and other embeddings may behave differently. We generated toxic text only to measure its toxicity, and we release risk scores and not the text. The target quantile moves materially only on RealToxicityPrompts and on MMLU at $\alpha=0.3$, and neither shows a gain over the frontier; a task with a different kind of risk, such as summarisation faithfulness, would strengthen the conclusions.
  \item \textbf{The synthetic generators and their constants.} The generators and the per-experiment constants (bandwidth, gate centre and slope) are those of the earlier version of this work and were chosen while the generators were being developed, so they are not independent of the generators. We did not tune any constant after seeing the results reported here, and the constants on the real streams follow rules fixed in advance (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
  \item \textbf{Theory.} Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} needs conditional independence of the risks given the features and applies at a fixed level; Corollary~\ref{cor:drift} needs a Lipschitz condition that we cannot check. The long-run result (Theorem~\ref{thm:longrun}) holds for any weights but says nothing about local behaviour. Proposition~\ref{prop:running} bounds the conditional violation probability at the running level, but we have no guarantee that the running level stays near $\alpha$ over a short window, and the window identity in Remark~\ref{rem:running} shows that this is what the local violation rate measures. We do not assume that our streams satisfy Assumption~\ref{ass:ci}: they are orderings of a fixed item set with one recorded risk per item, so Theorem~\ref{thm:cond} and Proposition~\ref{prop:running} are statements about the rule, not certificates for these streams.
  \item \textbf{Proxy streams.} The streams built from TruthfulQA, RealToxicityPrompts, SummEval and LongHalQA fields in the earlier version contain no model outputs and some are ordered by a field linked to the outcome. We report them in Appendix~\ref{app:proxy} and draw no conclusion from them.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty on the real streams.} Standard errors on the real streams are over orderings of one fixed set of items, so they measure ordering variability only, not variability across item sets or generations. The released risk scores are canonical: sampled continuations are not bit-reproducible across hardware.
  \item \textbf{The toxicity task.} The toxicity classifier is the ground truth, and $81\%$ of the risks lie below $0.01$, close to its floor; the $0.9$ quantile in the three lowest bands is in that range. Only abrupt block shifts have known boundaries, so post-shift violation is reported for them; smooth orderings (embedding direction, length) are reported with local violation only.
  \item \textbf{Weak localisation.} With the bandwidth set to the median pairwise distance, the kernel weights are close to uniform on all of the real streams (effective size \RtpKernelNeff{} of \PoolLen{} on RealToxicityPrompts and \MmluKernelNeff{} on MMLU), not only on RealToxicityPrompts, which limits what ``the kernel weights contribute least'' can mean. Sharper kernels do localise (Section~\ref{sec:res-bw}), and we report them in full. TruthfulQA multiple-choice accuracy of the small model is near chance, so that task is uninformative about weighting.
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} The density-ratio rule is a simplified stand-in for published methods, and L-ARC \citep{zecchin2024localized} was run with the authors' update and defaults but without their feature rescaling and with a warm start (Section~\ref{sec:res-larc}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to show that drift-aware weighting improves conformal risk control for foundation models under prompt shift. What we found is narrower. The online update provides the long-run guarantee for any weights; the weights and the gate change local validity by less than one percentage point and sharpness by up to $1.6$ points; a matched short window and DtACI are as good or better in most of our settings; and PromptShift-CRC performs like a mid-ranked window without having to choose one. Tests fixed in advance to give the weights a better chance (a sharper kernel, regimes that recur, a second moving target, a published localised competitor) show no consistent gain over the frontier. We state a conditional bound with a computable prompt-drift term, give an evaluation protocol for comparing such rules fairly, and release the code, the model outputs and the streams so that others can test the drift-aware idea on tasks where the upper tail of the risk moves with the prompt.
